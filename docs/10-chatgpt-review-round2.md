## Overall assessment

The revised plan is substantially stronger than the previous version.

The response correctly addressed several important criticisms rather than merely changing wording:

- decision-level consistency was removed;
- complementarity is no longer judged only by \(P_{cw}+P_{wc}\);
- DINOv2 versus DINOv2-Reg was demoted to an ablation;
- named forensic cue ontology and synthetic counterfactual machinery were deferred;
- the compute plan was corrected for an RTX 4080 with 16 GB VRAM;
- the core framing was changed from dual-evidence consistency to selective cross-foundation disagreement;
- the deployment plan now correctly distinguishes pre-VLM routing from post-VLM disagreement.

The current formulation is sufficiently clean to begin implementation of Stage 1.

However, four issues should still be resolved before treating the research plan as frozen.

# 1. Novelty is now defensible for a Q3-oriented paper, but the claim must remain narrow

The current candidate claim is:

> **Source-only calibrated cross-foundation disagreement for selective single-image face PAD under unseen domain and attack shifts.**

This is much safer than the original formulation.

However, the paper must not claim that any of the following are novel by themselves:

- cross-fitting;
- ensemble disagreement;
- selective prediction;
- uncertainty calibration;
- source-only model selection;
- using two different pretrained models.

These are standard ideas outside FAS and, increasingly, inside FAS.

The novelty should therefore be framed specifically around the PAD threat model:

\[
\boxed{
\text{heterogeneous foundation models}
+
\text{source-only pseudo-shift calibration}
+
\text{security-constrained selective PAD}
+
\text{conditional VLM inference}
}
\]

The paper should be presented as a **PAD method/system contribution**, not as a new general theory of uncertainty estimation.

A suitable positioning is:

> We study whether independently pretrained self-supervised visual and vision-language foundation models provide complementary errors for single-image face PAD, and whether source-only calibrated cross-model disagreement can improve selective security under domain and attack shifts.

This is narrow enough to remain defensible.

# 2. Cross-fitted risk calibration has a predictor-distribution mismatch that should be tested

In the MICO setting, suppose the target is \(T\) and the three source datasets are:

\[
A+B+C.
\]

The current domain-OOF risk-calibration procedure is approximately:

\[
B+C \rightarrow A,
\]

\[
A+C \rightarrow B,
\]

\[
A+B \rightarrow C.
\]

The held-out predictions from \(A,B,C\) are concatenated to train the risk calibrator.

However, the final branch predictors used on the real target are trained as:

\[
A+B+C \rightarrow T.
\]

Therefore the risk calibrator is trained on outputs generated by predictors trained on **two source domains**, while inference uses predictors trained on **three source domains**.

This is not necessarily invalid. Similar ideas appear in stacking and out-of-fold calibration.

But it creates a possible distribution mismatch:

\[
P(z_{\text{risk}} \mid \text{two-source model})
\neq
P(z_{\text{risk}} \mid \text{three-source final model}).
\]

A reviewer could reasonably ask whether the risk model is learning artifacts of weaker fold predictors instead of generalizable failure structure.

## Recommendation

Evaluate at least two cross-fitting strategies.

### A. Sample-level OOF

Split source subjects/videos into folds while preserving all three source domains in every training fold.

For example:

\[
(A+B+C)_{\text{train fold}}
\rightarrow
(A+B+C)_{\text{held-out subjects}}.
\]

This generates genuinely out-of-sample predictions while keeping the predictor distribution closer to the final \(A+B+C\)-trained model.

### B. Domain-OOF

Keep the current approach:

\[
B+C\rightarrow A,\quad
A+C\rightarrow B,\quad
A+B\rightarrow C.
\]

This is stronger as a pseudo-domain-shift simulation.

### C. Combined OOF

Potentially combine:

- ordinary sample-OOF records;
- domain-OOF records.

Then test whether the mixture provides better target-risk calibration.

The comparison itself could become a useful result:

> Does pseudo-domain-shift cross-fitting improve selective PAD more than ordinary sample cross-fitting?

If domain-OOF clearly wins on untouched targets, this strengthens the paper rather than weakening it.

# 3. SiW-M unseen-attack evaluation has a potential prompt-leakage problem

This is the most important protocol issue still remaining.

Suppose the held-out attack is:

\[
\text{silicone mask}.
\]

If the VLM has never seen silicone-mask images during FAS training, but the prompt bank contains:

> `"a silicone face mask"`

then the method is not operating under a fully unknown-attack setting.

It is closer to:

\[
\boxed{\text{open-vocabulary zero-shot attack recognition}}
\]

because the semantic identity of the supposedly unknown attack has been supplied explicitly through text.

This would make the claim "unknown attack detection" weaker.

The current protocol already states that the held-out attack cannot participate in prompt selection, but that is not sufficient if its class name or synonym is manually included in the prompt bank beforehand.

## Recommendation

Separate two evaluation settings.

### Protocol A — True unknown attack

If the held-out attack is silicone mask, the prompt bank must not contain:

- `"silicone mask"`
- `"latex mask"`
- an equivalent attack-specific synonym.

The semantic bank may contain only broader known concepts such as:

- bona fide / natural human face;
- print / paper presentation;
- replay / display presentation;
- artificial presentation;
- anomalous facial surface.

Then the system must detect the attack as unfamiliar without being told its exact semantic class.

This should be the primary unseen-attack protocol.

### Protocol B — Open-vocabulary attack recognition

Allow an explicit prompt such as:

> `"a silicone face mask"`

even though that attack has no image supervision.

This measures a different capability:

\[
\boxed{\text{zero-shot open-vocabulary semantic transfer}}
\]

It can be valuable, but it must be clearly separated from true unknown-attack detection.

Recommended paper structure:

- main table: true unknown;
- supplementary/secondary table: open-vocabulary unknown.

This distinction will make the evaluation much harder to criticize.

# 4. JS disagreement should be computed after independent branch calibration

The current basic disagreement score is:

\[
d(x)=D_{JS}(p_D\|p_V).
\]

However, DINO and the VLM may have very different intrinsic confidence scales.

For example, for the same correctly classified bona-fide sample:

\[
p_D(\text{live})=0.99,
\]

while:

\[
p_V(\text{live})=0.70.
\]

The raw JS divergence may be large even though the models conceptually agree.

That disagreement is partly caused by calibration scale rather than semantic disagreement.

## Recommendation

Calibrate each branch independently using only source validation data:

\[
\tilde p_D=C_D(p_D),
\]

\[
\tilde p_V=C_V(p_V).
\]

Use simple temperature scaling as the first baseline.

Then compute:

\[
d_{\text{cal}}(x)
=
D_{JS}(\tilde p_D\|\tilde p_V).
\]

Mandatory ablation:

\[
D_{JS}(p_D,p_V)
\]

versus:

\[
D_{JS}(\tilde p_D,\tilde p_V).
\]

Also compare against the simplest possible disagreement metric:

\[
|\tilde p_D-\tilde p_V|.
\]

If the simple absolute difference performs as well as JS, use the simpler method.

This will help prevent unnecessary complexity.

# 5. Recommended extension: make the selective-risk objective security-aware

The current risk calibrator estimates approximately:

\[
P(\text{prediction error}\mid z).
\]

For biometric PAD, however, errors are asymmetric.

A false accept:

\[
\text{attack}\rightarrow\text{bona fide}
\]

is generally much more serious from a security perspective than:

\[
\text{bona fide}\rightarrow\text{attack}.
\]

Therefore generic error probability may not be the most deployment-relevant target.

## Recommendation

Consider estimating separate risks:

\[
r_{FA}(x)
=
P(
y=\text{attack}
\mid
\hat y=\text{live},z
),
\]

and:

\[
r_{FR}(x)
=
P(
y=\text{live}
\mid
\hat y=\text{spoof},z
).
\]

Then define deployment policy under an explicit security constraint such as:

\[
APCER\le\alpha.
\]

Within that constraint, optimize:

- coverage;
- BPCER;
- VLM invocation rate;
- latency.

A highly practical evaluation question becomes:

> At a fixed APCER of 1%, what fraction of requests can be automatically decided before invoking the VLM or asking the user to retry?

This is more useful for a deployed biometric system than generic uncertainty AUROC alone.

It also differentiates the work from generic selective-classification papers.

# 6. Stage 1 is now ready to implement

The revised Stage 1 is appropriate:

1. frozen DINOv2-Reg + trained PAD head;
2. frozen CLIP/SigLIP + source-selected prompts/calibration;
3. fixed score averaging;
4. regularized learned score fusion.

Then evaluate:

\[
P_{cc},P_{cw},P_{wc},P_{ww},
\]

double-fault rate,

oracle gain,

error correlation,

and subgroup behavior.

The following order is recommended.

## Step 1 — Train the branches independently

### DINO branch

\[
x
\rightarrow
\text{DINOv2-B/14-Reg}
\rightarrow
\text{PAD head}.
\]

Use frozen backbone initially.

### VLM branch

\[
x
\rightarrow
\text{CLIP/SigLIP image encoder}
\]

with a fixed source-selected prompt bank and source-only calibration.

Do not jointly fine-tune the branches.

---

## Step 2 — Calibrate branch probabilities

Fit source-only calibration:

\[
p_D\rightarrow\tilde p_D,
\]

\[
p_V\rightarrow\tilde p_V.
\]

Store both raw and calibrated scores.

---

## Step 3 — Build the joint correctness table

For every untouched target sample record:

| DINO | VLM | category |
|---|---|---|
| correct | correct | \(P_{cc}\) |
| correct | wrong | \(P_{cw}\) |
| wrong | correct | \(P_{wc}\) |
| wrong | wrong | \(P_{ww}\) |

The key question is not merely whether:

\[
P_{cw}+P_{wc}>0.
\]

The project should continue only if:

- both branches have reasonable standalone performance;
- both \(P_{cw}\) and \(P_{wc}\) are non-trivial;
- \(P_{ww}\) is sufficiently low;
- oracle fusion provides a meaningful gain over the better branch;
- complementarity persists across more than one target or attack subgroup.

---

## Step 4 — Measure the oracle upper bound before building a risk model

If DINO achieves:

\[
AUC_D=95.5,
\]

VLM:

\[
AUC_V=92.0,
\]

and the oracle only reaches approximately:

\[
96.0,
\]

then there may be too little recoverable information to justify a second foundation model.

In contrast, if the VLM corrects a meaningful subset of DINO failures and oracle fusion produces a substantial gain, then the research hypothesis is worth pursuing.

This should remain a hard kill criterion.

# 7. Recommended final framing after the revision

The project should not return to ontology-heavy evidence consistency.

The strongest current formulation is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{independent DINOv2-Reg}
+
\text{independent VLM}
\rightarrow
\text{cross-foundation disagreement}
\rightarrow
\text{source-only selective risk}
\rightarrow
\text{security-aware accept / invoke-VLM / retry}
}
\]

Potential contribution statement:

1. **Cross-foundation complementarity analysis for PAD**  
   Quantify whether independently pretrained self-supervised visual and vision-language models recover different errors under domain and attack shifts.

2. **Source-only cross-fitted selective risk estimation**  
   Use only source-domain out-of-fold predictions to learn whether cross-model disagreement predicts failure on untouched target domains and unseen attacks.

3. **Security-aware conditional inference**  
   Evaluate whether the VLM can be invoked only when necessary while maintaining PAD operating constraints and improving the security-coverage-latency trade-off.

This is a cleaner Q3-oriented contribution than the original ontology/evidence-consistency formulation.

---

# Final recommendation

The research direction should continue.

The current revision has resolved the major structural problems from the previous version.

I do **not** recommend changing the overall direction again before Stage 1.

Before freezing the protocol, add the following four items:

1. compare sample-OOF versus domain-OOF risk calibration;
2. explicitly separate true unknown attacks from open-vocabulary unknown attacks;
3. calibrate DINO and VLM probabilities independently before computing disagreement;
4. evaluate security-aware selective risk under fixed APCER/BPCER operating constraints.

After those adjustments, the correct next step is implementation of the complementarity kill experiment.

The project should not invest in ontology learning, counterfactual cue synthesis, detailed heatmaps, or complex risk architectures until the Stage 1 oracle-gain experiment proves that the VLM contains recoverable information that DINO does not.
